<Chalk>say it out loud before it counts</Chalk>

<p class="lede">
Some conversations give you one shot: the job interview, the raise, the pitch. Sandeep
Swadia's fix is to rehearse them with an agent that plays the other person, out loud,
before the day arrives. The first time you say the thing that matters most should not
be in the room where it counts.
</p>

## <span class="sec-no">01</span>Say it out loud first

Interview nerves are close to universal. Swadia cites a survey by JDP in which 93
percent of people said they get anxious about job interviews, and the same survey's
useful finding: practicing the interview out loud is one of the best ways to bring
that anxiety down.

Out loud is the part most preparation skips. Notes and written answers polish what you
plan to say; they do nothing for the live experience of being asked, pausing, and
answering while someone waits. A rehearsal agent gives you that experience on demand,
as many times as you want, with nobody watching. The stakes stay at zero until the day
they are real.

## <span class="sec-no">02</span>Load the context, cast the role

The rehearsal is only as real as what the agent knows, so the setup starts with the
real materials: the company background, the job description, your resume, your cover
letter, anything you would want an actual interviewer to have read.
<a href="/guides/brief-the-model/">Brief the model like a brilliant new hire</a> is the
general form of this rule; here the new hire is playing your interviewer.

Then you cast the role. Swadia's brief: you are the hiring manager for a senior product
role, you are sharp, you are a little skeptical, and you have read my resume. Go ahead
and interview me. The brief also sets the rules of play:

- **One question at a time**, so it feels like a conversation instead of a
  questionnaire.
- **Push back on weak answers** the way a real interviewer would, instead of accepting
  everything politely.
- **Keep a record of the whole session**, so there is something concrete to review
  when it ends.

## <span class="sec-no">03</span>Talk, then break character

<PullQuote
  quote="don't type it. Talk to it. Turn on the voice mode"
  creator="Sandeep Swadia"
  video="4 AI Agents To Automate 99% Of Your Life"
  href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TL8V41Ea6oM"
  timestamp="17:11"
/>

Voice is what carries the practice into the real room. The major chat apps, Claude, ChatGPT, and
Gemini, all hold a spoken conversation on your phone: you can be interrupted, ask
clarifying questions, and hear yourself hesitate. Swadia runs his rehearsals on walks.

When the session ends, the agent changes jobs. Tell it: we are done with the interview,
now you are my interview coach. Then ask for the specifics a polite human would soften:

- Where was I weak, and where did I ramble?
- What should I have said?
- Give me three better ways to answer the hardest question.

The record it kept during the session is what makes this review concrete instead of
general encouragement.

## <span class="sec-no">04</span>Raise the stakes, keep the card

Once the friendly round goes well, make the next one harder. Give the agent a tougher
persona: the startup's CEO conducting your final round, interviewing you for
forty-five minutes and then giving you fifteen to ask your own questions, because that
part will happen in real life too. The technique scales as far as the stakes do.

<PullQuote
  quote="I know CEOs who rehearse their entire board meeting with six or seven agents"
  creator="Sandeep Swadia"
  video="4 AI Agents To Automate 99% Of Your Life"
  href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TL8V41Ea6oM"
  timestamp="16:07"
/>

Each of those agents is cast as one board member. Yours can be cast as anyone whose
questions you fear.

When the reps are done, ask for one last output: a one-page prep card of what you
learned, to read before the real conversation. The point of the card, and of every
round before it, is capacity: the ability to give a good answer to whatever gets
asked, the way a musician rehearses to be able to improvise on stage. A script breaks
the first time a question you never practiced arrives; the capacity survives.

<Exercise>
1. Pick a conversation in the next month where you get one shot: an interview, a
   review, a pitch. Gather the real documents the other side would have.
2. Write the persona brief: who the agent plays, its temperament, one question at a
   time, push back on weak answers, keep a record. Run fifteen minutes in voice mode
   on your phone.
3. Have it break character and coach you: weak spots, rambles, three better answers
   to the hardest question. Run one harder round, then ask for the one-page prep card.
</Exercise>
