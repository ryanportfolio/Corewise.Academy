<p class="lede">
The about page on this site is drawn as an architectural drawing set: numbered sheets, a title
block, line conventions declared on sheet A-001 and binding everywhere after. Nobody asked for
that. It came out of one true sentence about how the work was actually built: agents drafted,
an independent agent checked, a human approved. That is a drafting workflow, so the page became
a drawing set, and once it did, every design question that usually eats 30 minutes of meeting
answered itself. This guide is the method behind that page: how you get the next drawing set, for a
different subject, without copying this one.
</p>

## <span class="sec-no">01</span>Find the conceit that carries weight

Start with something true about the subject, not something pretty near it. The portfolio's
truth was procedural: specified, gated, checked, approved. The conceit, the single organizing
idea a design hangs on, is that truth given a body: a drawing set. This is the opposite of moodboarding, which starts from how things look and
works backward toward a justification.

Then apply the one test that matters: ask the conceit design questions it was never explicitly
built to answer. What typeface? A drawing set says monospaced and engineering-lettered, because
drawings are annotated, not typeset. What does red mean? Drafting convention reserves it for
markup and approval, so on this page red belongs to the human alone. What lives in the margins?
Title blocks, revision history, sheet numbers, all of which the page actually has. A
decorative one answers none of them; it sits on top of the page like a sticker. If the conceit
decorates but decides nothing, discard it and go find a truer sentence. The test costs 10
minutes and it is brutal, and it is the single biggest difference between a concept and a theme.

> “A load-bearing conceit answers question after question from its own internal logic.”

## <span class="sec-no">02</span>Write the contract before the layout

Before any layout work, write the semantic rules and make them binding. On the about page:
solid lines mean shipped and live, dashed means pruned or killed, phantom means unreleased, red
ink is reserved for human action. Those rules are published on the page itself, on the first
sheet, labeled binding for every sheet after.

The point of writing them down is the reframe it buys: once the contract exists, a violation is
a bug, not a matter of taste. A solid line on a killed project is now a factual error with a
correct fix, which means an agent can catch it, a reviewer can flag it, and nobody has to win
an argument about aesthetics. This is what makes the method work with AI in the loop at all.
Models are mediocre at taste and excellent at rule-following, so convert as much taste as
possible into rules. Turning that taste into instructions a model follows reliably is its own
craft, covered in <a href="/guides/brief-the-model/">Brief the model like a brilliant new hire</a>.
The reader never sees the contract as rules; they experience it as
coherence, the quiet sense that everything on the page is there on purpose.

> “Coherence reads as intent, and intent is precisely the thing slop lacks.”

Two more rules belong in the contract even though they are not visual. First, facts before
form: every number on the page gets verified at its source before it gets drawn, and a small
verify script rechecks 100% of the drawn claims, which makes the page carry its own eval harness. If you draw
eight of something, there are eight, countable. Second, real machinery: the figures on the
about page are projected 3D geometry computed in the browser, drawn by the same math that
produced the print edition, seeded so the output is reproducible. Generated-from-data survives
zooming in; a pasted texture
or a borrowed effect does not, and zooming in is exactly what a skeptical reader does.

## <span class="sec-no">03</span>Critique the render, three lenses minimum

When a draft renders, the pull toward "looks good, ship it" is strong and wrong. Schedule at
least 3 adversarial passes of 15 minutes each, every one with a distinct lens, and aim them at
the rendered output, never at the source. Code can be immaculate while the render is broken, and the render is the
only thing the reader receives. Keeping the critic separate from the maker generalizes well past
design; <a href="/guides/automate-the-bottleneck/">Automate the bottleneck, not the busywork</a>
argues for separating the evaluator from the worker so nothing grades its own output.

The lenses that earned their keep:

| Lens | The question it asks | The failure it catches |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Audience | What does a recruiter skimming for 30 seconds take away? | The right page for the wrong reader |
| Craft | Does the contract hold on every sheet? | Alignment, spacing rhythm, typographic drift |
| Anti-slop | Which of the tells below is present? | Decoration with no semantic job |

The anti-slop lens hunts a specific list of tells. Gradient glows nothing asked for. Decoration with no
semantic job. Perfectly symmetric filler content. Numbers that feel invented. Effects that fall
apart under zoom. Style imported from some other subject's truth. Each pass must return
findings. A pass that comes back clean did not certify the page; it failed, because every
render has real faults and a lens that found none was pointed in the wrong direction. Rerun it
narrower.

The last rule is the one that keeps the method alive: never carry the conceit to the next
subject. The drawing set worked because it was derived from this portfolio's truth. Reaching
for it again on a different subject is skipping step one, and skipping step one is the exact
move that produces slop, your own greatest hits pasted where they no longer mean anything. The
method transfers. The conceit never does. Next subject, next true sentence, next derivation.

<Exercise>
1. Take a page you need to design and write five true sentences about its subject, none of them
   visual. Pick the truest and propose a conceit from it. Then interrogate the conceit with five
   design questions it was not built for (typeface, color semantics, margins, motion, empty
   states). Give it 20 minutes, no more. Fewer than four confident answers, discard it and try
   the next sentence.
2. Write the constraint contract before touching layout: every visual element that carries
   meaning, and what it is forbidden to mean. Hand the contract to your agent with the build
   brief, then audit the render against it line by line and file each violation as a bug.
3. Run three critique passes on the rendered page with three lenses: your actual audience on a
   deadline, a typographer, and a slop-hunter armed with the tells list above. Each pass must
   produce written findings. Fix, re-render, repeat until the findings stop being structural.
</Exercise>
