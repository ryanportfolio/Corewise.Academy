<Chalk>a model that misses four times in five is still worth running</Chalk>

<p class="lede">
Some AI tools produce a good result only now and then. Ask one for a finished video from a
sentence of text and most attempts come back broken. That miss rate reads as a reason to give up.
The people getting usable output every day are running the same unreliable model, in a way that
turns the misses into a cost they planned for. Four moves do most of
the work, and while the example here is AI video, each move applies to any generator whose quality
swings from one run to the next.
</p>

## <span class="sec-no">01</span>Edit, don't generate

Asking a model to build a whole clip from a text prompt is the hard version of the job. It has to
invent every frame, keep them consistent across time, and get the lighting and motion right all at
once, and the small errors pile up until the result looks off. That is why so much AI video
disappoints.

The easier version hands the model something real to start from. Give it footage you already shot
and ask for a single change, and the pixels, the lighting, and the faces are already correct. The
model only has to insert the edit into a world that is already coherent.

<PullQuote
  quote="Rather than generate from total scratch … we're working in a medium that we've already recorded."
  creator="Nick Saraev"
  video="The BEST AI Video Strategy No One Is Using"
  href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7Su6W_FlUbk"
  timestamp="3:02"
/>

The same choice shows up far beyond video. Handing the model a draft to revise, a file to
refactor, or a design to adjust gives it a smaller, better-defined problem than a blank page. When
you can start from real material, start there.

## <span class="sec-no">02</span>Say the trigger and the change

A vague instruction gives the model room to wander, and it will. "Make it cooler" can mean a
hundred things, so you get one of the hundred at random. The output improves the moment you pin
down two things: the exact point where the edit should happen, and the exact edit.

Name the trigger, then name the change. "At 2.9 seconds, when he snaps his fingers, swap his shirt
for a black hoodie" tells the model the one moment to watch and the one thing to do. The trigger
can be a timestamp, an action, or a spoken word; the change is a single, concrete edit. That
specificity is what makes an unreliable model land the shot, and it is the same discipline behind
<a href="/guides/stop-shouting-at-the-model/">Stop shouting at the model</a>: precise beats loud.

## <span class="sec-no">03</span>Sample in parallel, then pick

Even with a sharp instruction, a single attempt lands maybe one time in five. The instinct is to
run it once, watch the result, dislike it, and run it again, and that loop makes you wait out a
full generation on every round.

<PullQuote
  quote="This is going to work like 20% of the time."
  creator="Nick Saraev"
  video="The BEST AI Video Strategy No One Is Using"
  href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7Su6W_FlUbk"
  timestamp="8:17"
/>

The faster loop generates several attempts at once and picks the best of the batch. You pay for
the miss rate once, up front, in cost and in a single wait, instead of paying it round by round.
Fire five, keep one. This is a general habit for any generator whose quality varies run to run: a
worker produces candidates, and a separate step selects. That split (a producer and an independent
picker) is the same shape as the evaluator in
<a href="/guides/automate-the-bottleneck/">Automate the step that slows you down</a>, and you can
hand the whole retry-and-collect loop to an agent so it runs the batch and gathers the candidates
for you.

## <span class="sec-no">04</span>Hide the seam

AI output carries a signature you can see. At the time of the source video the edited footage came
back at 720p, a lower resolution than the native shots around it, with slightly different
compression. Cut straight from that footage to your own high-quality footage of the same kind, a
talking head against a talking head, and the eye catches the jump.

<PullQuote
  quote="You need to hide the 720p seam with an intelligent cut to a different scene."
  creator="Nick Saraev"
  video="The BEST AI Video Strategy No One Is Using"
  href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7Su6W_FlUbk"
  timestamp="4:02"
/>

The fix is to cut to a different kind of shot instead. Move from the AI clip to a screen share or
other cutaway footage, and there is no matching thing beside it to measure the seam against, so the drop in
quality never registers. The principle holds for text too: an AI-written passage set right next to
your own voice reads as a seam, and a change of tone or format between them does the same job
as the cut.

<Exercise>
1. Take a task you would normally ask a model to produce from scratch and rewrite it as an edit.
   Find or make a rough starting artifact (a draft, a clip, a layout) and ask for one specific
   change to it instead. Compare the two results.
2. Rewrite one vague instruction as a trigger-and-change pair: the exact moment or condition, then
   the exact edit. Run both versions a few times and count how often each lands.
3. Pick a generator whose output varies run to run. Generate five outputs in one batch rather than
   one at a time, and choose the best. Note the total cost and time against what five serial
   retries would have taken.
</Exercise>
