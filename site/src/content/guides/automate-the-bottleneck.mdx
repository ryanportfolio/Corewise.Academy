<p class="lede">
The first instinct with automation is to aim it at whatever annoys you most. That instinct wastes
weeks. The people who build these systems for a living — Anthropic's own engineers, whose public
talks and interviews this guide distills — work in a different order: locate the constraint, solve
it once by hand, and only then encode the solution so it never needs solving again. Everything
else in this guide hangs off that ordering.
</p>

The logic comes from operations theory, and the classic picture is a scout troop on a trail. The
line stretches because the fast hikers speed ahead, but the troop arrives when the slowest hiker
arrives — so the leader moves the slowest walker to the front and redistributes the weight in his
pack. The troop speeds up precisely because the fastest hikers slowed down. Your workflows obey
the same arithmetic: an hour saved anywhere except the constraint is an hour that queues up
behind it, indistinguishable from an hour not saved at all.

## <span class="sec-no">01</span>Interrogate before you build

Most people cannot name their actual bottleneck, which is why the first tool is an interrogation,
not a build. Ask why the work is slow; ask why of the answer; keep going five layers down. Fifty
new sales leads are worthless if onboarding is manual — the leads were never the constraint. Only
when the constraint has a name do you choose an instrument for it, and the instrument should be
the smallest one that works: a skill for a repeatable procedure, a scheduled routine for a
recurring check, an artifact for something you need to see, an external tool when the work lives
outside the model. One problem, one deliberate pairing — not one hammer for everything.

## <span class="sec-no">02</span>Four parts of a machine that reports to you

If the interrogation reveals that *you* are the bottleneck — you dispatch every task, you chase
every status — the fix is structural. A synchronous, top-down loop (you ask, it answers) caps the
system at the speed of your attention. The working alternative is bottom-up: Claude initiates,
and you review what arrives. Every proactive system, human or machine, has the same four parts,
and naming them for your own workflow is most of the design work:

- **Trigger** — the cadence. Time-based ("Monday, 8 a.m.") or event-based, which in practice
  means a frequent, token-cheap check that decides whether the expensive work should run at all.
- **Worker** — whatever does the job. Build the minimum viable version; solve today's problem,
  not a theoretical future one.
- **Access** — the accounts and tools the worker may touch. The test is the same as for a new
  hire: does it have the logins the task actually requires?
- **Receipt** — the proof of completion, and the part most people get wrong. A receipt must be
  explicit, not implied: "141 emails reviewed" carries a verifiable number; "emails reviewed"
  hides every possible failure behind three words.

Anthropic runs this shape on real work — one internal system hands Claude the entire growth-
experiment loop on a schedule, with the analyzed results returning to the team as the receipt.
The pattern scales down just as well: one trigger, one worker, one explicit receipt, delivered
wherever you already look every day.

## <span class="sec-no">03</span>The receipt is not the story

A receipt is the total at the bottom of a dinner bill. The trace is the line items — the full
transcript of what the system actually did, step by step. Unwatched automations drift, and the
correction for drift is not more experiments or cleverer prompts. Asked for the secret at an
engineering workshop, Anthropic's applied-AI answer was disarmingly manual:

Reading traces is a three-step discipline. First, make sure the logs exist — scheduled runs
usually keep their conversation history, and anything you run elsewhere should write a run log
to a known location, ideally through one shared logging skill so the format can be changed in
one place. Second, actually look at them, in whatever viewer makes that effortless. Third — the
step that compounds — act on what you find: bring the trace back to Claude, point at the exact
wrong turn, and have it amend the skill itself. That converts a one-time failure into a
permanent repair, because the fix lands in the system rather than in the conversation.

## <span class="sec-no">04</span>Objectives, not tasks

Tasks are instructions: send this report, reply to these emails. Objectives are outcomes: make
this true, figure this out. The strongest colleagues you have ever worked with took objectives —
and Claude can too, but only inside the right scaffolding. The cautionary tale is a retro game
built two ways by Anthropic engineers: the same objective, prompted conversationally, shipped
something that *looked* finished until the first arrow-key press did nothing. Restructured — the
objective plus scaffolding, then a closed laptop — it came back six hours later fully playable.
Same model, same goal; only the structure changed.

That structure has three parts. Agree on what done looks like before any work starts, at
uncomfortable granularity — the engineers behind that experiment settled on twenty-seven
contract criteria, because vague criteria produce a shrug while granular ones point at the exact
line to fix. Add an evaluator that is not the worker, with real access to run the product,
because a model grades its own work the way an employee writes their own performance review.
And require the final receipt to carry proof — screenshots of the running thing, not a
paragraph asserting success.

<Exercise>
1. Pick the workflow that ate the most of your week. Write down why it is slow; ask why of that
   answer, five times. The last answer is your candidate bottleneck.
2. Design — on paper, before building anything — the four parts of a proactive system for it:
   trigger, worker, access, receipt. Make the receipt an explicit, countable claim.
3. Build the smallest worker that could possibly run once, run it, and read the entire trace
   line by line. Bring one wrong turn back to Claude and have it amend the skill so that class
   of failure cannot recur.
</Exercise>
