<p class="lede">
Set a learning goal you can check: a task you can do and explain. AI can supply
examples, question your reasoning, and find gaps in an attempt. You still need to
make the decisions that show you understand the work.
</p>

Austin Marchese's <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7DJk83sc5xg">The Art of
Becoming Self-Educated with Claude</a> starts with the task you need to do.
Nick Saraev's <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FSXHk4hMrY8">How
To Learn Hard Things Faster With AI</a> adds ten tutoring roles and repeated
practice. The original prompts below combine those ideas with source checks and
unaided attempts.

## <span class="sec-no">01</span>Choose a task you can check

Curiosity is enough reason to learn something. For a work skill, name the task
that learning should make possible. Marchese starts with the problem holding up
his business; <a href="/guides/automate-the-bottleneck/">Automate the step that slows you down</a>
covers finding that problem before choosing a tool.

Use these questions to keep the study tied to the task:

| Question | Write down |
| --- | --- |
| Why learn this? | The problem you need to solve |
| How much must I understand? | A task you can perform and explain |
| Who has done this before? | A source with relevant experience and evidence |
| How will I practice? | A small attempt with a checkable result |
| How does it fit here? | The team's current process and constraints |
| What should I learn next? | The specific gap the attempt exposed |

For example, replace a goal of learning spreadsheets with producing a weekly
sales total from an export, checking the total, and explaining how duplicate rows
affect it. That gives you a stopping condition and a way to choose useful lessons.

Have AI ask about your goal before it writes a curriculum. Then test what you
already know with a small task. Use the result to put needed basics first in your
plan, with something to attempt after each concept. Saraev's
<a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FSXHk4hMrY8&t=476s">interviewer and mapmaker
examples</a> use that sequence to turn a broad subject into a plan.

```text
I want to learn [skill] so I can [task] by [deadline]. Ask one question at a time about my goal, current knowledge, available study time, and the conditions in which I must perform. Wait for each answer.

Then give me a small task to assess my starting point. After I attempt it, propose a plan that puts needed basics first. For each part, name a source to check, a task to attempt, and a result that would show I can move on. Base my starting level on the attempt and flag uncertain judgments.
```

## <span class="sec-no">02</span>Give the tutor useful sources

Supply your goal, what you already understand, and an example of the work. Ask
for a plain explanation, then one applied to your task. The briefing method in
<a href="/guides/brief-the-model/">Brief the model like a brilliant new hire</a>
applies to tutoring too. Include the needed context in the session rather than
assuming the tool already knows your background.

Choose sources you can inspect: current documentation and a practitioner's worked
example. Ask where the example's conditions differ from yours. Adding documents
gives the model reference material; it does not train a new expert model.

Marchese uses NotebookLM for this source collection. Google's current
<a href="https://support.google.com/gemininotebook/answer/16179559?hl=en">help page,
titled Use chat in Gemini Notebook</a>, documents source selection and citations
that open the cited passage. Ask for answers from your selected sources and
inspect the citations before adopting a recommendation.

Open the passage supporting a key claim and check both its meaning and its fit
to your task. For a larger collection, <a href="/guides/build-a-knowledge-base-your-team-can-ask/">Build a knowledge base your team can ask</a>
covers retrieving relevant material.

Ask AI to find human explanations that match a demonstrated gap. Say what you can
already do and where your attempt failed, then ask which resource addresses that
missing step and why. Saraev recommends this
<a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FSXHk4hMrY8&t=1460s">search for explanations
at your level</a>. Open the suggested links and check their examples before using
them; a generated resource list can contain invented titles or links.

## <span class="sec-no">03</span>Choose the next tutoring job

Choose the tutor's job from the problem in your latest attempt. Saraev names ten
roles; these instructions keep each role tied to something you must do.

| Role | Use it when | Ask AI to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Interviewer | Your goal is vague | Ask about the task, deadline, and performance conditions |
| Mapmaker | You need a learning plan | Put needed basics first, with a task after each concept |
| Explainer | One step blocks you | Explain that step or give a hint, then let you retry |
| Socratic questioner | Your reasoning is uncertain | Ask why a decision works and what would change it |
| Examiner | You need to check your level | Ask one question at a time, increasing difficulty |
| Checker | You have work to inspect | Check intermediate steps and the result against a source |
| Listener | You can explain the idea yourself | Compare your explanation with the source and name gaps |
| Diagnostician | The same mistake returns | Propose a shared misunderstanding and a way to test it |
| Sparring partner | You must perform under pressure | Play the other person with realistic constraints |
| Clerk | Your own notes need organizing | Make an outline or cards while preserving uncertainties |

Attempt a small task yourself, then explain your decisions before reading AI's
feedback. If you get stuck, ask for help with that step.
After receiving help, restart the task without hints. Saraev emphasizes that
<a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FSXHk4hMrY8&t=705s">unaided restart</a>;
follow it with a changed example to check whether you can apply the idea elsewhere.

Recall practice can also strengthen memory. In
<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16507066/">Roediger and Karpicke's study of
prose passages</a>, prior recall tests improved delayed retention compared with
restudying. That finding supports testing yourself after a gap. It does not
establish how fast an AI tutor will teach a work skill.

Anthropic's <a href="https://www.anthropic.com/news/introducing-claude-for-education">Learning
mode announcement</a> describes asking learners to explain their approach and
support conclusions with evidence. The following original prompt requests that
questioning style in an ordinary chat; it does not enable a product mode.

```text
My task: [what I need to do]
Success check: [how I will check the result]
Sources: [documents or excerpts]
My attempt, intermediate steps, and explanation: [paste them]

Ask one question about a decision in my attempt, then wait for my answer. Let me try before giving a correction. If I am stuck, give one hint about that step and let me retry. Check my intermediate steps as well as the result; keep the work mine. Tie factual corrections to a supplied source and flag anything the sources cannot settle.

After we address the gap, have me restart without hints. Then give me a changed example, one question at a time. Increase difficulty when I can explain my answer. If I start guessing, name the missing concept and give me a smaller task that tests it.
```

Check the result against the task's success condition as well as the tutor's
feedback. In the spreadsheet example, calculate a small sample's total both with
your formula and by hand, then compare them. Treat the model's grade as feedback to
check. If the tutor disputes your answer, ask it to identify the step and the source
supporting its correction. Praise does not verify the total.

## <span class="sec-no">04</span>Find the misunderstanding

Keep your attempts and checked corrections. When the same error returns, ask AI
to look for a shared misunderstanding across that evidence. A proposed cause is a
guess to test with another task. Saraev's
<a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FSXHk4hMrY8&t=1127s">diagnostician role</a>
uses this approach to redirect study toward a missing concept.

Supply the relevant attempts in the session. A tutor may lack access to earlier
chats, and a remembered summary may omit the work that exposed the error.

```text
Here are my attempts, corrections checked against sources, and the source passages: [paste them].

Identify a misunderstanding that could explain repeated errors. Point to the attempts that support your guess and any that contradict it. Ask one new question that would test your explanation, then wait. If there is too little evidence, say what is missing.
```

You can also compare your own spoken explanation, written summary, or sketch with
the same source. Saraev describes
<a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FSXHk4hMrY8&t=1074s">making these different
versions himself</a>. Ask where each version misrepresents the source or leaves
out an important connection. If the
tool accepts only text, paste a speech transcript or describe the sketch. Have AI
check your explanation before it supplies a replacement.

## <span class="sec-no">05</span>Review mistakes over time

Turn checked mistakes into questions to answer again later. Ask the clerk to
organize your notes and draft cards, then inspect each answer against its source.
Keep unresolved questions labeled as unresolved.

```text
Organize these notes without adding facts: [paste notes]. Keep my uncertainties visible.

From the checked corrections, draft review cards. Each card needs one question, a short answer, and the source passage supporting it. Put uncertain answers in a separate list for me to check. Show the question before the answer during review and wait for my attempt.
```

Attempt the answer before revealing it. The
<a href="https://docs.ankiweb.net/background.html">Anki manual</a> describes this
recall method and scheduling reviews farther apart as recall becomes easier.
Use a review app or a calendar to bring you back after a gap. A chat's proposed
schedule still needs to be saved somewhere that will remind you.

Check performance with another changed task as well as cards. Remembering what a
formula means and using it correctly on an unfamiliar export are separate checks.

## <span class="sec-no">06</span>Practice under real conditions

Match practice to the setting where you must perform. For a live conversation,
ask AI to play the other person, challenge vague answers, and give feedback after
the round. Use your own timer when response time matters. For a full interview
routine, see <a href="/guides/rehearse-the-interview-with-an-ai-coach/">Rehearse the
interview with an AI coach</a>.

For practical work, have AI generate realistic inputs while you produce the
output. In the spreadsheet task, ask AI to present a synthetic export with
duplicate rows and wait until you have calculated the total before showing its
answer key. Check your total and its key against a hand calculation. Increase difficulty by changing the
data or constraints after you can explain the result.

Saraev also suggests
<a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FSXHk4hMrY8&t=1579s">building practice
apps</a>. If repeated setup is getting in the way, ask a coding agent for a small
app that presents a task, waits for your attempt, and records errors. Check its
tasks and answer key against your sources before trusting its scores. A
simulation gives you practice; real work is the next check.

## <span class="sec-no">07</span>Check the team's current process

Before applying the lesson to a shared process, write down its current steps and
ask the person who does the work to correct them. Marchese recommends a flowchart.
Mark any step you have assumed so the chart cannot quietly turn a guess into a fact.

Compare the proposed change with that checked process. Name who depends on the
result and what must stay compatible. If the problem itself is unclear,
<a href="/guides/take-a-problem-apart-before-ai-solves-it/">Take a problem apart before AI solves it</a>
covers separating facts from assumptions.

<Exercise>
1. Choose one work task. Write its success check and the decisions you need to explain.
2. Gather a relevant source and a worked example. Use the interview-and-plan prompt to assess your starting point and choose the first task.
3. Make an attempt, including your intermediate steps. Choose a tutoring role from the table and check its feedback against the source.
4. After a question, hint, or explanation, restart without help. Then attempt a changed example and check the output yourself.
5. Save errors and checked corrections. If an error repeats, use the diagnosis prompt and test its proposed cause with a new question.
6. Make checked review cards. Save a later review in your calendar or review app, then attempt both the cards and another changed task without hints when that review is due.
7. If you plan to change a shared process, check its current steps with the person who does the work. Record any remaining learning gap as your next study topic.
</Exercise>
