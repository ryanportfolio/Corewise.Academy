<p class="lede">
Claude Design is Anthropic's design tool, at claude.ai/design. Where a plain
chat answers a design request instantly, this one interviews you first, keeps
your brand's colors and fonts on file, and lets anyone on the team fix a draft
by circling the problem. Austin Marchese, a creator who uses Claude Code daily,
walked through seventeen uses of it in an Anthropic-sponsored video. This guide
distills them into one workflow.
</p>

## <span class="sec-no">01</span>Trained to ask design questions

Claude comes in several general-purpose products (the chat, Claude Code for
coding, Cowork for office work) and this specialized one. The difference shows
the moment you ask for something visual.
Marchese asks for a party flyer, and instead of producing one, Claude Design
asks who is hosting and what paper the flyer will print on. The same prompt in
a plain chat generates an image immediately and never asks.

If an asset deserves a designer's questions, it belongs here; for a quick
answer or working code, stay in a plain chat or Claude Code.

One limit to know going in: it is a layout tool, in the same category as Canva
or Figma, and it will not generate photos or images. Where a picture belongs,
it leaves a labeled empty box, a placeholder, for you to fill. Generate the
image elsewhere and drop it in; the design and the layout are what this tool
decides.

## <span class="sec-no">02</span>Start with a design system

The usual failure with AI-made assets is that every one starts from scratch:
you argue a report into the right look, and the next report forgets everything.
The fix is a design system, the written record of your brand's colors, fonts,
and layout rules that every later asset inherits.

In Claude Design that record is a built-in feature. Open the design system
tab, attach your website so it can read your existing branding, answer its
questions (or click "decide for me"), and it produces a system the whole
organization can use. It is the same move <a
href="/guides/one-skills-library-for-your-whole-team/">One skills library for
your whole team</a> makes with written procedures: build the standard once,
and everyone's output inherits it.

Once the system exists, it feeds four kinds of work:

- **Rebuild old documents.** Marchese uploads a client proposal made in Canva
  under his old branding and has it rebuilt in the new system, then tweaks it
  directly on the page. Any stale deck, one-pager, or PDF gets the same
  treatment.
- **Rebuild in the client's branding.** The same trick reversed: hand a prospect a
  proposal that looks like their own company made it. Give the tool your
  proposal and the client's site, and ask for the remake in their style.
- **Templates for the rest of the team.** A designer saves an Instagram
  graphic, weekly report, or proposal as a template inside the design system,
  and non-designers fill it in without breaking the brand.
- **Working email templates.** He shows it a plain login email, asks how to move
  the redesign into Supabase (the service that sends his login emails), and it
  rewrites the email with the per-user parts, like the login link, marked the
  way Supabase expects, ready to paste in.

## <span class="sec-no">03</span>Fan out variations, then pick

Describing a design change in words is slow and imprecise. Marchese's habit is
to make the tool show him options instead: ask for five variations of a landing
page, or of one piece of it like a header or a chart, pick the two he likes,
ask for five more in that direction, and converge over a few rounds. Five is
a starting point, not a rule.

Color gets the same treatment. Ask it to explore color and type pairings for
your channel or product, and it renders them on your actual design; a built-in
switcher lets you click between pairings and watch the page change. Seeing
options beats reasoning about them in the abstract, and clicking between
rendered versions beats editing code and reloading.

The tool can also decide how an asset gets used. He has it model a 3D version
of his logo, likes it, and has no idea what to do with it, so he asks the tool
to put it on his website in a few realistic ways. It returns three options and
he picks the slowly rotating version. When you have an asset and no plan, ask
the tool how it would use it. What makes an option good rather than merely
different is its own craft; <a
href="/guides/the-design-concept-does-the-work/">The design concept does the
work</a> covers where design quality comes from.

## <span class="sec-no">04</span>Wireframe before you build

A wireframe is a clickable mock-up: it looks and feels like the product, but
nothing behind the buttons is real. At Marchese's last startup, pitches to
artists like Ed Sheeran were wireframes, because clicking through a fake
product persuades better than pictures of one, at a fraction of the
engineering cost.

Claude Design makes them from a prompt. He describes a workout-plan product,
lets the tool decide the open questions, and gets screens he can click through
to feel how the app would work. Coding agents have made real products cheaper
to build, but building one is still the expensive way to test an idea; a
wireframe is often enough. When a design settles, connect your code base and
have the tool port it over as the starting point for the real build.

## <span class="sec-no">05</span>Comments the AI acts on

Feedback on a design usually degrades into words about pictures: screenshots,
arrows, "a bit higher". Claude Design keeps the feedback on the design itself.
Marchese reviews a website draft, adds a comment telling the header to span two
lines, draws a circle around a graphic where he wants it moved, then clicks
"send to Claude", and the tool works through the comments and fixes them.

The same mechanism carries a team. Share the design and teammates comment
directly on it; nobody installs anything, pulls the latest code, or trades
screenshots. A reviewer with no technical background can mark up the draft the
way they would mark up paper, and the AI applies the changes.

## <span class="sec-no">06</span>Edit it until it reads like you

Everyone has a backlog of assets that never get made because they are nobody's
job: the how-to video you wish existed, the onboarding explainer for a new
teammate, the one-page PDF version of something you published, the resume
untouched for eight years. Marchese makes several on camera: an animated
explainer of his product, a branded PDF companion to a video, a resume
rebuilt from his LinkedIn. His rule of thumb: use the generated version where
the stakes are low and the asset would otherwise not exist; a product launch
still deserves exact, by-hand control.

The reason generated assets are often embarrassing to send has a name in his
telling: doom prompting. You go back and forth with a chat, it never quite
lands, you give up and export the near-miss anyway.

Claude Design's answer is editing in place: click into the draft and rewrite
the text directly on the page until it sounds like you, no more prompting
round-trips. The design carries your brand because of the system; the words
carry your voice because the final pass over them was yours. For why generated
output drifts into slop in the first place, and how to catch it in text too,
see <a href="/guides/why-ai-makes-slop-and-how-to-catch-it/">Why AI makes slop
and how to catch it</a>.

<Exercise>
1. Go to claude.ai/design, open the design system tab, and build your design
   system: attach your existing website for the branding, answer the questions
   you care about, and click "decide for me" for the rest.
2. Pick one stale document (an old proposal, deck, or one-pager), upload it,
   and have it rebuilt in the new system. Fix the details by editing directly
   on the page.
3. Take one piece of the result and ask for five variations. Pick the two
   you like, ask for five more like those, and notice how fast you converge.
4. Share the finished draft with one teammate and have them leave a comment
   and a drawn markup. Click "send to Claude" and check what it fixed.
</Exercise>
